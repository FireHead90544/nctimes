A forty-year-old retail name was three quarters from insolvency when Nikhil Chandra was brought in — not to rebrand it, as the board initially wanted, but to first establish whether any of its stores were actually profitable on their own terms.

<Photo variant="ph-d" label="Photo: Store Relaunch Day" caption="The relaunch of the first restructured store format, Mumbai flagship." alt="Store relaunch day" />

The answer, after six weeks of store-level economics work, was that three of the eleven stores were profitable, four were breaking even, and four were being quietly carried by the others. The board had known this, in a general way, for years. Nobody had put a number on it.

Putting a number on it changed the conversation. What had been framed as a brand problem — an outdated identity in a changed market — turned out to be a real estate and operations problem. The brand was fine. The store economics were not.

<Video label="▶ Client Recap Reel — 2:14" caption="A short internal recap, filmed for training use, summarising the fourteen-month turnaround." />

The turnaround plan closed four stores, rebuilt the operations model for the seven that remained, and deferred the brand refresh until the economics were stable enough to support it. Fourteen months later, the brand posted its first profitable year since 2019.

The brand refresh happened after that, on the back of a profitable business rather than as an attempt to rescue one. It worked better, for the obvious reason: people respond to a brand that is actually doing well.
