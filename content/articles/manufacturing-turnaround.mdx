A family-run manufacturer was losing share quietly, for years, before anyone agreed to call it a crisis. By the time Nikhil Chandra was called in, the conversation had shifted from "how do we grow" to "how do we stop losing."

<Photo variant="ph-e" label="Photo: Floor Walkthrough, Pune" caption="A floor-level audit during week two of the engagement, before any recommendations were drafted." alt="Floor-level audit at the manufacturing facility" />

The diagnostic began on the floor, not in the boardroom. Two weeks of operations audit before a single client presentation. What the audit found was not a strategy problem but a scheduling and supplier coordination problem that had been compounding for four years.

Delivery delays were the symptom. The constraint was a scheduling system that had not been updated since the firm was a third of its current size. The supplier relationships that had worked at that scale were now a bottleneck. Nobody had drawn the connection between the two.

The fix was unglamorous: a rebuilt scheduling model, a renegotiated supplier base, and a new operations manager hired specifically for the coordination role that the founder had been filling informally. None of this required a strategy deck. It required an honest conversation about what the firm's growth had actually done to its operations.

<InfoBox title="Engagement Results">
- **Delivery delays** — Reduced by 60% within six months
- **Supplier base** — Consolidated from 24 to 11 partners
- **Operations hire** — First dedicated operations manager in firm history
- **Revenue** — Grew 2.8x over four years post-engagement
</InfoBox>

The engagement is now cited internally as the flagship case for the five-step diagnostic method — the one Nikhil Chandra uses first when explaining to a new client what the process looks like when it works. It is unglamorous as a story. It is the most useful one he has.
