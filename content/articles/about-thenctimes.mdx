A portfolio is, structurally, an advertisement. A newspaper is, structurally, an argument that something happened and is worth your attention. Nikhil Chandra wanted the second thing more than the first.

So TheNCTimes borrows the conventions wholesale — kickers, decks, bylines, datelines, cross-references — not as decoration but as discipline. If an entry can't be written as a dispatch a stranger would want to finish reading, it doesn't get a slot.

The format also enforces honesty about time. Every article is dated. You can see what happened in what order. There is no ability to quietly update the headline on a failed project and present it as a success. The archive is what it is.

<PullQuote cite="Nikhil Chandra, Editor">
A portfolio that grows by adding dispatches — each one dated, filed, cross-referenced — is itself a demonstration of the discipline I ask of clients.
</PullQuote>

The newspaper metaphor runs deeper than aesthetics. A newspaper makes editorial choices: what gets the front page, what gets buried on page four, what gets a two-column headline versus a single. Those choices reveal what the editor actually thinks is important. TheNCTimes works the same way.

What gets the front page is the founding story and the foundational principles — not the awards, not the client list. The awards are on page three. The contact details are in the classifieds. The editorial choices are deliberate, and they are meant to be read.
