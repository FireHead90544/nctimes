Across forty-odd engagements, Nikhil Chandra has held to three rules that, he says, have never once produced a bad outcome — even when they made the room uncomfortable.

<PullQuote cite="Nikhil Chandra">
If I can't explain the recommendation to a first-year mentee without flinching, I haven't earned the right to bill for it.
</PullQuote>

The first rule is sequencing: no fix before the constraint is proven. This sounds obvious until you are sitting across from a founder who has already decided what the answer is and would like you to confirm it quickly. The diagnostic always comes before the deck. Without exception.

The second is honesty about uncertainty, even when a client would rather hear confidence. There is a version of consulting that sells certainty it does not have. Nikhil Chandra has watched that version of consulting fail, repeatedly, in slow motion, and has no interest in it.

The third is simpler than it sounds: he will not take on work he would be embarrassed to describe to someone learning the job. This is not a moral test. It is a quality test. If the work cannot be explained clearly to a mentee, it is probably not good work.

All three have cost him engagements. None, he says, has cost him a client twice. The clients who leave because of the diagnostic usually come back when the thing they wanted confirmed turns out not to be true. The ones who don't come back were not the right clients.

<InfoBox title="The Three Rules">
1. **No fix before the constraint is proven** — diagnostic always precedes recommendation
2. **No false confidence** — uncertainty is named, not papered over
3. **The mentee test** — if you can't explain it simply, don't do it
</InfoBox>

He is asked, occasionally, whether holding to these rules makes consulting harder. The honest answer is yes, sometimes, in the short run. In the longer run, the answer is no — because the work that results from following them is the only kind worth doing.
