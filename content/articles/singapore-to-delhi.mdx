A regional retailer's expansion into India needed more than a translated playbook — it needed one rebuilt from the ground up, starting with assumptions about supply chains that simply did not transfer.

What looked, on paper, like a market-entry exercise turned into a nine-month rebuild of the operating model itself.

<Photo variant="ph-c" label="Photo: Client Site Visit, Singapore" caption="Early scoping sessions at the client's Singapore headquarters, January 2026." alt="Early scoping sessions at client headquarters in Singapore" />

The original brief was to adapt an existing retail format for the Indian market. The client had done this successfully in three Southeast Asian markets and expected a similar timeline. What they found, in the first six weeks of diagnostic work, was that the assumptions underpinning the format — about supplier relationships, about last-mile logistics, about customer expectation of service level — all required reconstruction rather than adaptation.

This is a common pattern in cross-border work. Companies that have succeeded in expanding within a region often arrive with a confidence that the next expansion will follow the same shape. It rarely does.

<PullQuote cite="Nikhil Chandra">
The hardest part of cross-border work is not the regulation. It is convincing a client to throw out a deck they spent three months building.
</PullQuote>

Nikhil Chandra credits most of what worked to a willingness, on both sides, to throw out the original deck entirely. The resulting entry strategy was built on entirely different unit economics, a different store format, and a different supplier base. It worked. The original plan would not have.
