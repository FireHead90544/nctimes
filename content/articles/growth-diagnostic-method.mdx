Every engagement Nikhil Chandra takes on begins the same way: not with a strategy deck, but with a five-step diagnostic designed to surface the real constraint before anyone proposes a fix.

The steps are unglamorous on purpose — data audit, constraint mapping, stakeholder triangulation, a single prioritised hypothesis, and only then a plan. Skipping a step, in his experience, is the single most common cause of an expensive recommendation that nobody implements.

<InfoBox title="The Five Steps">
1. **Data Audit** — What do we actually know, and how reliable is it?
2. **Constraint Mapping** — What is the single binding constraint on growth?
3. **Stakeholder Triangulation** — Who knows something the data doesn't show?
4. **Hypothesis** — One specific, testable statement about the constraint
5. **Plan** — A sequence of actions against the constraint, not against the symptoms
</InfoBox>

The diagnostic takes, typically, two to four weeks. Clients who arrive hoping to compress this into one week almost always slow the overall engagement down by six. The time saved at the front is spent, with interest, correcting for the wrong constraint.

<PullQuote cite="Nikhil Chandra">
A good constraint statement is two sentences long. If it takes a slide deck to describe the problem, the problem hasn't been found yet.
</PullQuote>

The method is not proprietary and is not a secret. Nikhil Chandra teaches it to his mentorship cohort and describes it freely in talks. Its value is not in the knowledge of the steps but in the discipline of executing them in order and treating each gate seriously.

Several clients have adopted versions of it internally after engagements have closed. This is, by his account, the best possible outcome: the diagnostic outliving the engagement.
