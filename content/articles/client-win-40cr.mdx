An eighteen-month mandate with a mid-cap manufacturer became the template Nikhil Chandra now reaches for first whenever a new client describes growth as the goal but cannot say, specifically, growth in what.

The brief had arrived vague, as most do. What followed was four weeks of diagnostic work before a single recommendation was made — a sequencing Nikhil Chandra insists on regardless of how impatient a client's board might be.

<Photo variant="ph-a" label="Photo: Strategy Workshop, Gurgaon" caption="A working session with the client's leadership team during month four of the engagement." alt="Strategy workshop with client leadership team" />

The diagnostic revealed three things: that the company's growth constraint was not, as the board believed, a sales problem; that two of its five product lines were quietly subsidising the other three; and that the one person in the organisation who already knew this had been told, three years earlier, that it wasn't a useful observation.

The resulting plan did three things: cut the underperforming lines, rebuilt the incentive structure for the remaining two, and created a direct channel that had previously been dismissed as too expensive. Revenue scaled meaningfully. Headcount did not blow out.

<InfoBox title="Engagement Snapshot">
- **Duration** — 18 months
- **Sector** — Mid-cap manufacturing
- **Constraint found** — Product line economics, not sales
- **Result** — Revenue growth without headcount expansion
- **Legacy** — Diagnostic reused on 6+ subsequent mandates
</InfoBox>

The engagement produced a five-step diagnostic that has since been used, with modification, on more than a dozen other mandates. The modifications are always small. The sequencing is always the same. Constraint first. Always.
